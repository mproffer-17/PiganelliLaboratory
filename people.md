# Production Crew

The Piganelli Lab is a collaborative research group at Indiana University School of Medicine studying type 1 diabetes, redox immunology, T cell metabolism, beta cell stress, and early immune biomarkers.

    <h3>About Jon</h3>

    <p>
      Jon leads the scientific direction of the Piganelli Lab and has built his research program around a central question: why does the immune system lose tolerance to the insulin-producing beta cell, and where can that process be interrupted?
    </p>

    <p>
      His research spans T cell-mediated beta cell destruction, redox signaling, oxidative stress, immunometabolism, beta cell stress, and therapeutic strategies aimed at slowing or preventing type 1 diabetes. That broad background allows projects in the lab to move between molecular mechanisms, cellular immunology, animal models, and translational questions.
    </p>

    <p>
      As the lab's “Executive Producer,” Jon connects those different projects into the larger scientific story. He is heavily involved in experimental brainstorming, grant writing, mentoring, collaborations, and finding ways to turn unexpected results into the next research question.
    </p>

    <p>
      Outside the formal science, Jon helps give the lab much of its personality. Classic rock and golden-oldies playlists regularly make their way into the lab, discussions can easily detour into astrophysics, and lab members frequently acquire memorable nicknames along the way.
    </p>

    <h3>About Roy</h3>

    <p>
      Roy brings together a broad scientific background spanning parasitology, host-pathogen biology, immunology, autoimmune disease, and translational biomarker research. That range is especially valuable when a project sits at the intersection of infection, cellular stress, and immune activation.
    </p>

    <p>
      In the Piganelli Lab, his work focuses on early immune events associated with type 1 diabetes, including beta cell stress, viral influences on autoimmunity, neoantigen formation, autoreactive T cell responses, and biomarkers that may identify disease-associated immune activation before clinical diabetes develops.

    </p>

  <div class="scientist-description">
    <h3>About Megan</h3>

    <p>
      Megan is a PhD student studying some of the earliest immune changes that occur during the development of type 1 diabetes. Her research asks how autoreactive CD4+ T cells become activated, how their antigen-reactivity patterns evolve over time, and whether those early events leave measurable signals that can be detected before overt disease.
    </p>

    <p>
      Her work combines spontaneous and adoptive-transfer mouse models with antigen-specific tetramer analysis, multiparameter flow cytometry, beta cell stress measurements, soluble biomarkers, and human translational samples. A central focus of her research is soluble LAG-3 as a potential marker of an early window of T cell activation.
    </p>

    <p>
      Megan is also interested in antigen diversification and the relationship between beta cell stress and expanding autoreactive T cell responses. Her projects are designed around following disease longitudinally rather than looking only at its final clinical endpoint.
    </p>

    <p>
      Beyond the bench, she contributes to figure and poster design, scientific writing, website development, lab organization, and outreach projects that make the lab's science more accessible.
    </p>
